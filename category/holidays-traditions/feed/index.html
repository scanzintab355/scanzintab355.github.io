The Date and Its Deep Roots: Why January 7th?

The calendar debate isn’t just trivia; it’s a story of faith and identity. After the Soviet Union tried to suppress religious holidays, many Russians clung to the Julian calendar’s Christmas as a quiet act of cultural preservation. Now, it’s a proud tradition.

Orthodox Christmas vs. Western Christmas

Orthodox churches, including the Russian Orthodox Church, follow the Julian calendar, which is currently 13 days behind the Gregorian calendar. So, while much of the world unwraps gifts on December 25th, Russians pull out their decorations and start celebrating on January 7th.

In the 20th century, Soviet authorities suppressed religious holidays, pushing New Year’s Eve as the main winter festivity. But since the fall of the USSR, Russian Christmas has seen a powerful comeback, blending old rituals with modern celebrations.

What Actually Happens on Russian Christmas Day?

Forget the flashy, commercialized Christmas you might expect. Russian Christmas is a measured, soulful affair focused on faith, family, and folklore.

The Twelve-Dish Supper

The absolute heart of the celebration is the “Holy Supper” on the evening of January 6th, which kicks off the Christmas feast. Families serve twelve meatless dishes—each symbolizing one of the twelve apostles.

  • Beetroot soup (borscht)
  • Kasha (porridge)
  • Stuffed cabbage rolls
  • Fish dishes
  • Honey and nuts

This meal is not just about eating; it’s a spiritual cleanse, a ritual of hope and gratitude before Christmas Day dawns.

Church Services and Carols

Midnight mass is a massive deal. Churches fill up with worshippers dressed in their best, filling the air with ancient chants and prayers. Carolling, or “kolyadki,” follows—a tradition where groups go door-to-door singing and spreading festive cheer.

It’s like walking through a living museum where every song, every ritual echoes centuries of faith and joy.

Practical Tips for Experiencing Russian Christmas 2026

  1. Plan for the cold: Russian winters are no joke. Bundle up in layers—wool scarves and boots are your best friends.
  2. Join a kolyadki group: If you get the chance, join locals in caroling—it’s the best way to feel the festive pulse.
  3. Try the Holy Supper: Even if you’re not Orthodox, sampling the twelve dishes gives you a taste of history and faith.
  4. Respect the rituals: Churches are packed, and the atmosphere is solemn. Observe quietly and respectfully.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why do Russians celebrate Christmas in January?

Russian Orthodox Christians use the Julian calendar, which runs 13 days behind the Gregorian calendar, placing Christmas on January 7th.

What is the significance of the twelve dishes at the Holy Supper?

The twelve dishes symbolize the twelve apostles. The meal is meatless and focused on grains, vegetables, and fish, reflecting fasting traditions and spiritual preparation.

Is Russian Christmas a public holiday?

Yes. January 7th is an official public holiday in Russia, and many businesses close to allow families to celebrate.

How does Russian Christmas differ from New Year’s Eve celebrations?

New Year’s Eve in Russia is loud, festive, and secular, with fireworks and parties. Christmas is quieter, religious, and family-centered.
